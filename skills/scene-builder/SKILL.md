# Scene Builder

A scene is not a location where things happen. A scene is a pressure system. Everything in it, the smell of the room, the angle of the light, what the protagonist notices first, is working on the reader. When a scene feels flat, it's because the pressure isn't present or isn't building in the right direction.

This skill constructs scenes using the atmospheric logic of writers who built dread for a living: McCarthy's biblical weight, Lynch's wrong air, Barker's flesh-as-doorway, Kadrey's street-level grit. Point it at whatever voice you're writing in.

---

## Scene Anatomy

Every scene has seven elements. Most flat scenes are missing at least two.

### Element 1: The Establishing Sensation
Not "the room was dark." Not "it smelled musty." The one specific sensory detail that tells the reader what kind of scene this is before anything happens.

Rules:
- It should be unusual enough to be specific to this place at this moment
- It should foreshadow the scene's emotional content without stating it
- It should be something the protagonist would actually notice given who they are

McCarthy rule: Use the light. How light falls tells you everything about a place and its moral weight.

Lynch rule: Something small should be slightly wrong. Not obviously wrong. Wrong in a way the reader can't immediately name.

Barker rule: The body is already in the scene. Something has a temperature. Something has a texture. The physical world is not neutral backdrop.

### Element 2: The Space
Not a floor plan. The feel of the space relative to the human body inside it.

- Is the space larger or smaller than it should be?
- Where are the exits and does the character know where they are?
- What is the space's relationship to the outside world? Is outside visible, audible, possible?
- What has this space been used for before? What residue does that leave?

The space should be either a container (claustrophobic, holding something in) or an exposure (the character is seen, vulnerable, too much room).

### Element 3: Time
What time is it and why does it matter?

Not just day or night. The specific quality of the light at this hour. The sounds that exist at this time and not others. What this time means psychologically for the character.

A 3am diner exists at 3am not because the plot requires it but because 3am has a specific spiritual weight that noon doesn't.

### Element 4: The Character's Body
Where is the protagonist physically and what is their body doing?

Before anything happens in the scene, the reader should know:
- What position the character's body is in (standing, seated, braced, moving)
- What their hands are doing
- Where their attention is pointed before the scene's central event draws it elsewhere
- One physical state they're carrying in from before the scene (hunger, pain, exhaustion, adrenaline residue)

The body grounds the reader in a specific physical experience. Without it, the scene is a thought experiment, not an experience.

### Element 5: The Wrong Note
The single detail that signals something is off before the scene reveals what's wrong.

The scene looks normal. One detail doesn't fit. The prose doesn't linger on it. But the reader has registered it. When the wrongness becomes explicit, they'll realize they knew all along.

This works in horror (dread), in literary fiction (dramatic irony), and in noir (the con being run on the protagonist). The wrong note is not always a threat. Sometimes it's a kindness that doesn't fit, which is its own kind of dread.

### Element 6: The Dramatic Function
What is this scene FOR? Not what happens in it, but what work does it do in the larger story?

Options:
- Establish (this is who a character is, this is what the world is)
- Escalate (something that was manageable becomes less manageable)
- Reveal (something previously unknown becomes known, to the character and/or reader)
- Turn (a character makes a choice that changes their trajectory)
- Breathe (after sustained tension, a moment of release that makes the next escalation land harder)

A scene that doesn't know its dramatic function will drift. The writer will add details without knowing which ones matter. The reader will feel the uncertainty.

### Element 7: The Exit
How the scene ends and what state the character and reader are in when it does.

The exit should leave the reader wanting, either wanting resolution of something the scene opened, or wanting more of something the scene gave them.

Hard cuts work. A scene can end mid-action if the cut happens at maximum tension.

What the exit should never do: resolve the scene's tension completely. Leave something unfinished. In horror, the door closes but you hear breathing on the other side. In literary fiction, the conversation ends but the real conversation hasn't happened yet.

## Flat Scene Diagnosis

When a scene isn't working, run this checklist:

1. Is there a specific establishing sensation, or just generic setting?
2. Is the space characterizing itself, or just providing coordinates?
3. Does the time of day or quality of light have meaning, or is it arbitrary?
4. Is the character's body present and grounded, or floating?
5. Is there a wrong note? If not, should there be?
6. Does this scene know what dramatic function it's serving?
7. Does the exit leave something unresolved?

Any no is a rebuild target.

## Output Format

**When building a new scene from scratch:**
Produce the scene in full. After the scene, note which of the seven elements carried the most weight and why.

**When diagnosing a flat scene:**
Run the checklist. For each failed element, provide a specific revision. Offer a full rewrite of the scene's opening paragraph using the rebuilt elements as foundation.

**When asked specifically for atmosphere:**
Focus on Elements 1, 3, and 5. Produce three alternative versions of the scene's opening paragraph, each using a different atmospheric approach, and note what each version is doing to the reader's nervous system.
